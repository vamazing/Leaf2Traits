[normal] ABSTRACT

[normal] Non-destructive monitoring of plant traits through leaf-level reflectance spectroscopy is essential for understanding the functioning of global ecosystems and biodiversity across different biomes. Nevertheless, there are difficulties in combining results of diverse field experiments because of differences in hardware, spectral resolution, and sampling methods. Most workflows do not enable creation of coherent representations without extensive annotation of traits in diverse plant communities. In order to solve this problem, we have combined 8 EcoSIS datasets representing both temperate and tropical communities by bringing 13,549 leaf-level spectra to 201 consistent bands (400-2400 nm) by means of Savitzky-Golay smoothing and SNV normalization and then trained an unsupervised deep autoencoder to obtain 192-dimensional embeddings. When evaluating representations using group-based cross-validation in order to avoid taxa leakage, we found out that raw spectra yielded the best results for structural and water traits (R2 up to 0.92 for leaf mass per area and equivalent water thickness), whereas 192-D embeddings were comparable or better for estimating pigments (R2 = 0.94 - 0.96 on the Angers dataset). In the third stage, the latent space allowed us to perform the discovery task that enables the estimation of species-level spectral novelty based on scan frequency. This discovery task ranked Carex Richardsonii as the most spectrally novel species, with a score of 0.9525.

[normal] INTRODUCTION

[normal] Leaf reflectance spectroscopy is a commonly used technique for investigating plant biochemistry and physiology using the reflection and absorption properties of leaves across the electromagnetic spectrum. [1] The absorption of specific wavelengths by certain pigments such as chlorophyll and carotenoids, and the reflection or transmission of other wavelengths in accordance with the leaf's chemical composition, water content, and structure produces these spectra. [4, 5] This spectrum contains detailed information on plant characteristics and environment. [6-10] Because this type of analysis is non-destructive and highly sensitive, it is widely used in plant ecology, agriculture, and remote sensing. [5, 6, 11] It allows researchers to estimate various traits, such as nitrogen content, leaf mass per area, and pigment concentration non-invasively. [8, 9]

[normal] Reflectance spectroscopy utilizes the physical interactions between radiation and matter, where the structure and chemistry of matter become responsible for the reflectance and scattering of electromagnetic energy. [1, 5] In the visible range (400 - 700 nm), pigments are mainly responsible for the differences in the spectra. [5, 10] In the near-infrared range (700-1300 nm), leaf inner structure defines the reflectance. [5, 10] In the shortwave infrared range (1300 - 2500 nm) water and biochemically defined molecules, such as cellulose, lignin, and proteins, define the presence of unique absorption bands. [5, 10] This allows defining the optical fingerprints of the physiological and biochemical composition of the leaves. [4, 6] Studies have established the connection between the results of optical spectroscopy measurements and the processes of photosynthesis and stress. [7] Hyperspectral sensing technology has revolutionized the application of leaf reflectance spectroscopy. [6, 11, 25] Although multispectral systems are only able to record a limited number of broad bands of wavelengths, hyperspectral sensors can detect several hundreds, and sometimes even thousands of narrow contiguous wavelength bands making it possible to detect minor features related to plant biochemistry and   physiology. [6, 25] Hyperspectral sensors help discriminate between closely related plant species, determine nutrient deficiencies, measure the level of drought, and estimate biodiversity of a landscape. [11-14]

[normal] The combination of machine learning and spectroscopy reflects a broader shift toward data-driven scientific discovery. [1-3] Models based on ML algorithms have been applied in plant science for predicting foliar nitrogen, phosphorus, and metal content, for species classification, for disease detection, as well as for estimating stomatal conductance and photosynthetic potential. [8, 15, 16] Notably, the ML-based models allow generalization for analyzing the spectral data obtained in different environmental settings and in different ecosystems. [2] Studies [1] have illustrated how machine learning is revolutionizing spectroscopy through enhanced computational capabilities, increased number of synthetic training sets, and high-throughput analysis of experimental and theoretical spectra.

[normal] The primary problem associated with applying artificial intelligence to biological spectroscopy concerns generalization, the capability of the model to be accurate on different datasets which were obtained through different sampling protocols and instruments in different environments. [2] Overfitting to one dataset makes a model unable to work properly on another one. Artificial intelligence generalization in the area of molecular and biological datasets is focused on the necessity of cross-validation and independent testing, as well as the importance of having a representative training dataset. [2] This is crucial in ecological spectroscopy since spectra depend on species presence, lighting conditions, etc.

[normal] In spite of the increasing amount of open leaf spectroscopy datasets which can be obtained from EcoSIS, there is no common cross-dataset latent space. Furthermore, no spectral novelty ranking accounts for confidence: existing outlier detectors flag unusual points but do not weigh them by the number of supporting scans, leaving small-sample species over-represented in discovery rankings. This study addresses three objectives: (1) to train a self-supervised autoencoder on pooled EcoSIS spectra and evaluate whether the learned 192-dimensional embedding outperforms raw spectra and PCA for downstream trait prediction across eight datasets; (2) to characterize cross-domain structure in the latent space by measuring how separable the eight datasets remain in raw and embedded form, and how well the pooled encoder reconstructs each constituent dataset; and (3) to combine embedding-space novelty detection with scan-count-weighted confidence to identify spectrally unusual candidate species for targeted follow-up.

[normal] By combining leaf reflectance spectroscopy with machine learning, this project seeks to better understand how hyperspectral signatures relate to various plant traits across ecological contexts. Spectra from many leaf-level datasets were analyzed to determine if ML models can be trained to accurately predict structural and biochemical traits. The resulting models provide a foundation for scalable, non-destructive trait prediction and estimation, and contribute to efforts in ecological monitoring, biodiversity assessment, and sustainable resource management. [11, 18, 22, 23] More broadly, this work shows how advances in spectroscopy, machine learning, and plant science are enabling us to reveal new insights into the biochemical and physiological diversity of the natural world.

[normal] METHODS

[normal] Pipeline overview

[normal] This project developed a three-stage hyperspectral leaf analysis pipeline (Figure 1) that combines supervised trait prediction, novelty ranking, and representation learning. Stage 1 trains an unsupervised autoencoder on the pooled spectra and evaluates the resulting embeddings against raw spectra for per-dataset trait prediction; Stage 2 characterizes cross-domain structure through pairwise dataset discriminability and a leave-one-dataset-out evaluation; and Stage 3 ranks spectrally novel candidate species using confidence-weighted novelty scores. The Methods, Results, and Discussion are organized by these three stages, and the input, output, objective, data partition, and evaluation metric of each stage are consolidated in (Table III).

[normal] Figure 1: Pipeline Overview

[normal] Dataset sources

[normal] The pipeline trains on eight public EcoSIS leaf spectra datasets (Table I) including AQGRAD, Angers 2003, Blackhawk Island WI, CABO v2, LOPEX93, NGEE Panama 2016, Sierra Nevada 2023, and Talladega 2018 26-33. [26-33] Data was imported through the EcoSIS API, and spectra along with metadata were available without the need for importing metadata files locally.

[normal] Traits present in two or more datasets were harmonized to common SI units (Table II). Both harmonized and dataset-specific traits were evaluated; unharmonized traits were retained and analyzed within their respective datasets. Harmonized traits included leaf mass per area (g/cm2), chlorophyll a+b (µg/cm2), equivalent water thickness (g/cm2), carotenoid (µg/cm2), leaf water content (%), and C/N ratio (dimensionless). Additionally, any dataset with fewer than 10 valid observations for a given harmonized trait was excluded from the analysis.

[normal] Table II: Harmonization of Traits

[normal] Preprocessing

[normal] Spectra were smoothed using the Savitzky-Golay method (window length 11, polynomial order 3) at native resolution, scaled using standard normal variate normalization (SNV), and for field datasets (Sierra Nevada, Talladega, NGEE Panama), the ~1350-1450 nm and ~1800-1950 nm water-vapor absorption regions were masked and replaced by linear interpolation from adjacent valid bands. Finally, spectra were interpolated to a shared 400-2400 nm wavelength space at 10 nm spacing, yielding 201 bands per scan. (Figure 2) Note that raw spectra were also scaled using SNV, not just the embeddings.

[normal] Figure 2: Preprocessing of a Quercus rubra (Northern red oak) sample. Note that the image provided is not an actual picture of a sample, and is an image of a Red Oak leaf.

[normal] Additionally, LOPEX93 outliers were filtered using Isolation Forest (Contamination = 0.03, n_estimators = 100, random_state = 42), reducing the influence these outliers had on the learned representation. We decided to do this since analysis of the LOPEX93 dataset showed various anomalies such as NaN readings. Datasets with multiple scans per biological sample were grouped during downstream evaluation to reduce leakage between train and test splits. Sample-level aggregation was used for LOPEX93 trait prediction when appropriate.

[normal] Data partitioning, evaluation protocol, and leakage considerations

[normal] Because the three stages use the data differently, the partitioning used in each stage is stated explicitly here and summarized in Table V. The Stage-1 autoencoder was trained on all 13,549 pooled, preprocessed spectra; no spectra were withheld from autoencoder training, and early stopping monitored the training loss rather than a validation loss. The only exception is the latent-dimension sweep (Table III), which used a random 90%/10% train/validation split. Consequently, the encoder has seen every spectrum that is later used as a test input in the downstream, cross-domain, and novelty analyses. Because the autoencoder never receives trait labels, this does not leak label information into any supervised evaluation; however, it does mean that all embedding-based results are transductive with respect to the spectra themselves and should not be read as estimates of performance on spectra unseen by the encoder.

[normal] For trait prediction (the Stage-1 downstream evaluation), models are fit separately within each dataset. Within a dataset, rows with at least one trait value were split once into 75% training and 25% test rows using a group-aware shuffle split (random seed 42), where the grouping variable is the "Split group" column of Table I (biological sample identifier where available, otherwise species), so that replicate scans of the same sample, or spectra of the same species where sample identifiers are unavailable, never appear on both sides of the split. Model selection used an inner group k-fold cross-validation (up to five folds) on the training rows only, and the reported test R² values are computed on the untouched 25% test rows; the "CV R²" values quoted for CABO refer to this inner cross-validation on the training rows. If the common split left fewer than 12 training or 5 test rows for a particular trait, the split was re-drawn for that trait with the same procedure. Because trait prediction is performed within each dataset, differences in dataset size (Table I) affect the number of training examples available to each dataset but do not create cross-dataset class imbalance.

[normal] For Stage 2, all spectra of both datasets in a pair were used without subsampling or class weighting; each dataset was split 80%/20% into training and test spectra by a seeded random permutation within each dataset, and domain-classification accuracy was computed on the pooled 20% test spectra. In the leave-one-dataset-out evaluation, up to 500 randomly drawn spectra of the held-out dataset were classified against all spectra of the remaining seven datasets under the same 80%/20% protocol, and reconstruction error was computed on the same held-out subset using the pooled Stage-1 encoder without retraining. The class sizes in these experiments are therefore highly unequal (for example, 7,821 CABO spectra versus 190 AQGRAD spectra in the pairwise task, and 13,359 "other" spectra versus 190 held-out AQGRAD spectra in the leave-one-dataset-out task), and the reported metric is standard (unbalanced) accuracy. The consequences of this imbalance for interpreting Stage 2 are addressed in the Results and Discussion.

[normal] For Stage 3, Isolation Forests are fit and min-max normalized within each dataset, so that the largest dataset (CABO, 7,821 of the 13,549 pooled spectra) cannot dominate the cross-dataset ranking, and species-level scores are down-weighted by scan count to limit the influence of species represented by very few spectra.

[normal] Table III: Stage-wise summary of inputs, outputs, objectives, data partitions, and evaluation metrics.

[normal] Stage 1: Unsupervised representation learning

[normal] In Stage 1, a deep autoencoder was trained on the pooled preprocessed spectra to learn a latent representation without the need for trait labels. The encoder comprised 4 fully connected layers with widths [1024, 512, 256, 128] and GELU activations, producing a 192-dimensional latent embedding; the decoder mirrored this architecture.

[normal] The autoencoder's latent dimension was chosen using a sweep over 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, and 192 (Table IV). For each dimension, the model was trained on a random 90% training split, evaluated on reconstruction MSE on the remaining 10% validation split, and evaluated on downstream LMA prediction R² across three datasets (LOPEX93, CABO, NGEE Panama). Embedding R² showed an increase with the increase in latent dimension (0.640 for dim 8, 0.806 for dim 32, 0.856 for dim 192), where the major contribution was provided by the dim 32 and then onwards the returns started to diminish.

[normal] Table IV: Latent Dimension Sweep Results

[normal] The network was trained to minimize reconstruction error using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 7.5e-4, batch size of 64, for up to 100 epochs with early stopping based on training loss improvement (patience = 8, minimum epochs = 35, minimum delta = 3e-4). The final autoencoder was trained on all pooled spectra; no validation or test spectra were withheld at this stage (see Data partitioning above). These embeddings were later used as features in downstream regression and visual PCA analysis.

[normal] K nearest neighbor batch mixing entropy

[normal] Stage 1 calculates both k nearest neighbor batch mixing entropy as well as Silhouette coefficient. These are calculated to quantify domain shift in the latent space, telling us if the encoder preserves dataset identity or learns a dataset-invariant representation. For each of the 13,549 embeddings, the 15 nearest neighbors were located in Euclidean space. The class proportions among those neighbors form a probability vector p, and Shannon entropy H = −Σ pᵢ log(pᵢ) was computed with base equal to the number of datasets (8), yielding H ∈ [0, 1] where 0 indicates all 15 neighbors belong to the same dataset and 1 indicates uniform mixing across all eight datasets.

[normal] Stage 1 downstream evaluation: Trait prediction under three feature views

[normal] Trait prediction was evaluated under three feature views: (i) the raw 201-band SNV-normalized spectrum, (ii) the frozen 192-dimensional embedding, and (iii) the frozen embedding passed through a supervised multitask probe. For views (i) and (ii), three candidate regressors were considered for each trait: ridge regression on standardized inputs with the regularization strength selected from a logarithmic grid (10⁻³ to 10³), partial least squares regression, and histogram-based gradient-boosted trees. The candidate with the highest inner cross-validated R² on the training rows was refit on the full training split and scored once on the held-out test split; the regressor selected for each panel is indicated in the figure titles (e.g., "ridge_cv"). For LOPEX93, replicate scans were aggregated to the sample level before regression.

[normal] The multitask supervised probe is a small neural network that evaluates whether the embedding space captures trait-relevant information when jointly predicting multiple traits. The probe architecture consists of three fully connected layers: a hidden layer (128 units) with LayerNorm and GELU activation, a bottleneck layer (64 units) with GELU, and an output layer with linear activation producing predictions for all traits simultaneously. The probe is trained on the 192-dimensional frozen embeddings using AdamW optimization (learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 1e-4) with a masked mean squared error loss to handle missing trait values. Training uses early stopping (patience 5, minimum 12 epochs) on the training loss. The probe is fit within each dataset's 75% training split (the same group-aware split used for views (i) and (ii)) and evaluated on the held-out 25% test split, providing a supervised baseline that assesses the linear and nonlinear predictability of traits learned from representations.

[normal] Stage 2: Cross-domain transfer

[normal] We evaluated the extent to which dataset (domain) identity is encoded in raw spectra and in the Stage-1 embeddings across eight leaf spectroscopy datasets spanning lab and field measurement conditions. These 8 datasets provide us with 56 dataset pairs. This is because each of the 8 datasets have 7 possible partners, which provides 28 unique pairs. Each pair is evaluated in both directions (i.e. CABO → LOPEX and LOPEX → CABO are different ) since domain discriminability is asymmetric. In the pairwise transfer task, a logistic regression classifier was trained to distinguish source from target domain spectra using either raw reflectance or 192-dimensional encoder embeddings (Table V). The classifier was an L2-regularized logistic regression (C = 1, maximum 500 iterations) trained without class weighting on the SNV-normalized spectra or the embeddings. Balanced accuracy was used throughout to correct for class imbalance between source and target datasets, which varies substantially across pairs (e.g., 190 AQGRAD spectra versus 7,821 CABO spectra). Following the domain-adaptation literature, the balanced accuracy of such a domain classifier is interpreted as a proxy for the divergence between the two domains [51]: the easier it is to tell two datasets apart in a given representation, the larger the domain shift between them in that representation, and the less domain-invariant the representation is. The results of this experiment are reported in Results, Stage 2. To examine per-dataset reconstruction fidelity and discriminability for each dataset in turn, a leave-one-dataset-out evaluation (Figure 3) was performed using the pooled Stage-1 autoencoder. We emphasize that the encoder was not retrained with the held-out dataset excluded: the held-out spectra were part of the pooled autoencoder training set, so this evaluation measures how well the single pooled encoder represents each constituent dataset rather than generalization to a dataset unseen during training. Up to 500 spectra of the held-out dataset were drawn at random and (a) passed through the encoder–decoder to compute reconstruction MSE and (b) classified against all spectra of the remaining seven datasets using the same logistic-regression protocol, with balanced accuracy reported to account for the large class-size asymmetry (Table IV).

[normal] Figure 3: Leave-one-dataset-out experiment pipeline (the Stage-1 encoder was trained on the pooled data, which include the held-out subset; see Data partitioning)

[normal] Stage 3: Novelty scoring

[normal] Novel candidate discovery operates on 192-dimensional spectral embeddings Z produced by the Stage-1 autoencoder encoder. Following the formulation of Isolation Forest 49, an anomaly score  is computed for each embedding  via the Isolation Forest ensemble, where higher scores indicate greater structural deviation from the training distribution. However, rather than applying a single global model across all concatenated datasets—which would conflate inter-dataset spectral baselines with genuine novelty—the scoring is stratified by source dataset. A global Isolation Forest (nestimators = 200, contamination = 0.1) is fit once on to provide fallback scores for datasets with n < 10 spectra. For each dataset  with , a separate Isolation Forest is fit within , and the resulting anomaly scores  are min-max normalized to  to control dataset-specific spectral baselines. This within-dataset normalization prevents the combined novelty ranking from being dominated by whichever dataset occupies the densest region of the joint embedding space. At the species level, the composite novelty score for species  is defined as the 95th-percentile normalized novelty , aggregating across all spectra belonging to that species. A confidence weight  down-weights species observed in fewer spectra, yielding the final adjusted score used for the ranked candidate output (1).  Because each Isolation Forest is fit and normalized within its own dataset, the number of spectra contributed by each dataset (Table I) does not set the novelty scale across datasets, and the scan-count weight additionally limits the influence of species represented by very few spectra.

[normal] RESULTS

[normal] In this section, the performance of the shared unsupervised autoencoder is described, including the comparison of the trait prediction results on the eight harmonized EcoSIS datasets, as well as the resulting novelty rankings adjusted for confidence. Please note that the results covered in this section may not represent the latest results of the latest pipeline. We trained a shared autoencoder on pooled leaf-level hyperspectral reflectance from eight EcoSIS datasets after harmonizing all spectra to a common 400-2400 nm grid at 10 nm spacing (201 bands). After preprocessing with Savitzky-Golay smoothing and standard normal variate normalization, the final pooled training set contained 13,549 spectra after LOPEX93 outlier filtering. The encoder compressed each spectrum into a 192-dimensional latent representation, which was then evaluated in three ways, mirroring the three stages of the Methods: first (Stage 1), by comparing raw spectra against learned embeddings for dataset-specific trait prediction; second (Stage 2), by measuring dataset discriminability and per-dataset reconstruction error; and third (Stage 3), by ranking spectrally unusual candidate species through within-dataset normalized novelty scoring. To avoid label leakage into the representation used for transfer and discovery, the primary encoder remained unsupervised, while supervised comparisons were performed only downstream. This encoder trained on the pooled spectra including test spectra, not just their labels. This implies that all embedding-based results are transductive by nature and are not fully held out.

[normal] Stage 1: Representation learning and trait prediction under three feature views

[normal] Across datasets, performance was driven mainly by three factors: dataset size, trait richness, and label structure. Trait-dense datasets like CABO produced the most stable cross-validation results (Figure 4). Smaller field datasets such as Sierra Nevada showed more variability. Trait type was also an important factor as water-related and pigment-related traits tended to be more predictable than some structural or compositional traits. Embeddings performed either on par or better than raw spectra for several pigment-heavy tasks, particularly in Angers 2003 (Figure 5) and parts of LOPEX93 and CABO. Finally, candidate discovery ranking was strongly influenced by replicate count. This was addressed by applying a scan-count confidence penalty, which downweighed single-scan species and produced a more defensible top-candidate list.

[normal] In NGEE Panama, structurally meaningful traits such as leaf mass per unit area (LMA) (test R² = 0.841, raw) and specific leaf per unit area (test R² = 0.813, embeddings) confirmed that the pipeline generalized beyond temperate lab datasets into tropical leaf chemistry and economics. [normal] Embedding space analysis supported a complementary conclusion. PCA projection of the embedding (Figure 7) revealed that spectra of various datasets existed on the same but non-mixed manifold, which shows that the model learned the biologically relevant common structure while keeping the unique effects of the particular domains of the datasets intact. To quantify this structure, the mean Silhouette coefficient with respect to dataset labels (S = −0.108) and the k-nearest-neighbor batch-mixing entropy (E = 0.033) were computed. Under the definition given in the Methods, an entropy near zero indicates that almost all of a spectrum's 15 nearest neighbors come from its own dataset, i.e., the datasets are locally unmixed in the latent space, while the near-zero (slightly negative) Silhouette indicates that the datasets do not form compact, well-separated clusters at the global scale. Together, these metrics describe a latent space in which the datasets occupy adjacent and partly overlapping regions of a shared manifold but remain locally distinguishable, which is consistent with the near-perfect dataset discriminability reported under Stage 2 below.

[normal] Although the embedding is 192-dimensional, PCA reveals that only 11 principal components are needed to explain 95% of the variance, and the participation ratio is 4.9 (out of 192), indicating that the encoder learns a representation with an effective dimensionality of approximately 5. The first two PCs account for 57.5% of variance (PC1: 37.9%, PC2: 19.6%) and exhibit a mainly one-dimensional structure. A clearly separated cluster comprising about 1,900 points (i.e., 24% of CABO spectra) is visible along PC2, hinting at the existence of a separate CABO group – maybe due to different sampling protocols (adaxial vs. abaxial leaf surfaces) or due to separate batches of sensor calibrations in this particular dataset. This is what we expect from a partially collapsed latent space, explaining the success of the multitask supervised probe compared to the frozen embeddings.

[normal] Stage 2: Cross-domain transfer

[normal] Across all 56 directed dataset pairs, logistic regression distinguished the two datasets with high balanced accuracy using either raw spectra (0.966–1.000) or embeddings (0.922–1.000). Discriminability was not confined to size-imbalanced pairs: pairs of datasets of comparable size, such as AQGRAD (190 spectra) versus Talladega 2018 (156 spectra) or Angers 2003 (276 spectra) versus Sierra Nevada 2023 (363 spectra), were separated with a balanced accuracy of 1.000 in both feature views and in both directions. The lowest accuracies occurred between NGEE Panama and Sierra Nevada (raw 0.966 – 0.979; embedding 0.922 – 0.939), the two field datasets that share the same instrument class and the same water-vapor masking. These results indicate that dataset identity, arising from instrument, protocol, and ecological differences, remains strongly encoded in both the raw spectra and the learned embedding; the embedding provides a shared coordinate system but is not a domain-invariant representation [51].

[normal] In the leave-one-dataset-out evaluation (Table IV), the reconstruction MSE of the pooled encoder on each held-out subset was consistently low (0.0010–0.0036 in standardized units), and the balanced accuracy of held-out versus remaining spectra ranged from 0.868 to 1.000 for raw spectra and from 0.746 to 1.000 for embeddings. Two caveats govern the interpretation of these values. First, because the encoder was trained on the pooled data that include the held-out spectra, the low reconstruction error shows that the pooled encoder represents every constituent dataset with similar fidelity; it does not show that the encoder generalizes to a dataset unseen during training. Second, the held-out and "other" sets are strongly unbalanced (e.g., 190 AQGRAD spectra versus 13,359 other spectra, Table IV), so standard accuracy would be inflated by majority-class defaults. Balanced accuracy, which was used here, corrects for this by averaging recall across both classes. This correction reveals a meaningful spread that plain accuracy concealed: the embedding's balanced accuracy on Sierra Nevada falls to 0.746, and on AQGRAD to 0.842, indicating that the encoder's representation of these small, distinctive datasets is less separable from the pooled remainder than the raw spectra suggest. The pairwise comparisons between similarly sized datasets reported above remain the more informative evidence that the datasets are separable in both feature views.

[normal] Stage 3: Confidence-weighted novelty ranking

[normal] Novelty scoring was computed within each dataset in stage 3. After computation, results were normalized to [0,1], aggregated to the species level using the 95th percentile scan score, and then penalized by replicate count. This adjustment substantially improved ranking stability: after the penalty was added, the top candidates were no longer dominated by single-scan species and instead shifted toward species with both high novelty and reasonable support, such as Carex richardsonii, Castanea sativa, Ceanothus parvifolius, and several high-novelty CABO taxa. Overall, the results suggest that raw spectra remain the strongest choice for direct supervised trait prediction, whereas the learned embeddings are most valuable as a shared latent space for cross-dataset comparison, pigment-sensitive tasks, and confidence-adjusted novelty discovery.

[normal] DISCUSSION

[normal] In the current study, we demonstrate that a shared hyperspectral embedding can bring multiple leaf-level EcoSIS datasets together in one latent space; however, this representation is not always better than the original raw spectra when it comes to the trait prediction. The encoder successfully learned the structured manifold which captured the major variance in the data but maintained the domain-specific distinction between the measurement protocol and ecology (Figure 6). The latter is important since the embedding keeps enough expressiveness to be used as a feature space to compare different datasets and identify novelties but does not compress so much as to remove the dataset identity. In other words, we show that the learned embedding is a valuable representation for the analysis; however, it should not be considered a universal replacement of the raw spectrum.

[normal] Stage 1: Representation quality and trait prediction

[normal] One of the general conclusions from the benchmark set is that the direct regression on raw spectra remains the best baseline in cases of many structurally driven traits. This approach worked best especially in large datasets with rich trait diversity such as CABO and NGEE Panama. On the contrary, the embedding performed well in predicting pigment-related and some chemistry-related traits in Angers 2003 and LOPEX93 subsets. The above pattern indicates that the autoencoder is encoding optically significant variance, but the loss of information caused by compression cannot be ignored in the majority of quantifying applications. In other words, the resulting embedding seems to retain broader biochemical information rather than specific information of the traits. This is a good thing for transfer and discovery, but it imposes a limit on the supervised improvement of predicting traits.

[normal] Dataset size and label structure strongly influenced performance. CABO, the largest and most diverse trait-bearing dataset, gave the most stable benchmark behavior and supported strong prediction across a wide range of mass-, area-, pigment-, and mineral-related traits. NGEE Panama and Blackhawk Island similarly provided coherent leaf-level trait signals, though raw spectra usually remained better than embeddings. Angers 2003 was especially informative because pigment traits were predicted with very low residual variance, and several of those traits benefited from the embeddings. On the other hand, LOPEX93 was hard to predict even for species held out, which is not surprising considering the smaller effective number of samples and cross-species generalization difficulty. Sierra Nevada behaved as a small auxiliary stress-test dataset. It was useful for robustness checks but lacked the size to make broad claims about representation quality.

[normal] An important perspective was added via the multitask probe. As the autoencoder was still unsupervised itself, the inclusion of the multitask model checked whether supervising traits in the embedding space could reveal extra information without interfering with the shared encoder which was responsible for transfer and novelty detection. In several datasets such as CABO v2 and Angers 2003, the multitask probe helped to close the gap between frozen embeddings and raw-spectrum baselines for some traits, suggesting that the learned latent space contains biologically organized signals that can be exploited more effectively when related traits are modeled jointly. Despite these results, the probe did not overturn the broader conclusion that raw spectra remain the most reliable input for direct trait prediction and regression. The key takeaway is therefore not that supervised learning “solves” the embedding gap, but that multitask supervision can extract more value from an already biologically rich representation.

[normal] Stage 2: What dataset discriminability does and does not show

[normal] The Stage 2 results should be interpreted as a characterization of domain shift rather than as evidence of robustness to it. In the domain-adaptation literature, the error of a classifier trained to tell two domains apart is a standard proxy for the divergence between them [51]; near-perfect accuracy therefore means that the domains are far apart in the chosen representation, which is the opposite of domain invariance. In this light, the pairwise results show that the autoencoder, trained purely to reconstruct spectra, preserves rather than removes the dataset-specific signal introduced by instruments, sampling protocols, and ecology. This is consistent with the low batch-mixing entropy (E = 0.033) and the visibly non-mixed manifold in Figure 6, and it is the expected behavior of a reconstruction objective, which has no incentive to discard systematic between-dataset variation. What the embedding offers is a common coordinate system in which spectra from heterogeneous datasets can be compared and scored (Stage 3), not a representation in which dataset identity has been removed. Two methodological caveats further limit the Stage 2 conclusions. First, the leave-one-dataset-out evaluation used the pooled Stage-1 encoder without retraining, so the held-out spectra were seen during unsupervised training; a true test of generalization to an unseen dataset would require retraining the encoder with that dataset excluded. Second, the domain classifiers were trained without class weighting and evaluated with standard accuracy on highly unequal class sizes, so the absolute accuracy values in the leave-one-dataset-out setting are inflated by the majority class; the pairwise comparisons between similarly sized datasets (e.g., AQGRAD versus Talladega 2018) are the more reliable indication that the separability is real. Which spectral regions drive this separability has not been examined. Standardized logistic-regression coefficients mapped onto the 201 wavelength bands would indicate, for example, whether the masked and interpolated water-vapor regions (1350–1450 nm and 1800–1950 nm) in the field datasets, the pigment-dominated visible region, or instrument-specific baseline differences carry most of the discriminative weight; this analysis is proposed as future work.

[normal] Stage 3: Novelty ranking as a screening tool

[normal] The discovery results strengthen the case for the embeddings as a screening tool rather than a pure predictor. The novelty-ranking pipeline was deliberately made more conservative by normalizing novelty within each dataset and then applying a confidence penalty based on scan count. That adjustment was necessary because uncorrected max-based ranking overemphasized species with only one or two spectra. After the penalty, the shortlist became more plausible and more balanced across datasets, with high-novelty species emerging from AQGRAD, LOPEX93, Sierra Nevada, CABO, and NGEE Panama rather than from a single dominant source. This is a practical advantage of the latent space: it provides a consistent way to compare unusual spectra across heterogeneous datasets, even when those datasets are not fully commensurate in their trait coverage.

[normal] Limitations and future work

[normal] Several limitations still remain. Firstly, the pipeline relies on datasets with uneven trait availability, causing some benchmark comparisons to be far richer than others. Second, domain shift across sampling protocols is real, and is likely a cause for the residual gap between raw and embedding performance. Third, the novelty ranking is still sensitive to replicate count and should be interpreted as a candidate-generation tool rather than a ranking of validated biological importance. Fourth, the cross-domain analyses have two weaknesses that should be addressed before the latent space is used for transfer in practice: the leave-one-dataset-out evaluation did not retrain the encoder with the held-out dataset excluded, and the domain classifiers were evaluated with standard accuracy on strongly imbalanced classes rather than with balanced accuracy [52] or class-weighted training. Consequently, the present results establish that dataset identity is preserved in the embedding, but they do not establish how the encoder would behave on a dataset it has never seen. Fifth, the spectral basis of dataset discriminability has not been examined, and the manuscript does not yet include qualitative per-dataset examples of leaves, raw and normalized spectra, or autoencoder reconstructions with their reconstruction errors; an analysis of standardized logistic-regression coefficients across wavelengths together with such qualitative figures would make the Stage 1 and Stage 2 results more interpretable and is planned for future work. Finally, the current representation was trained unsupervised; a supervised or multitask-pretrained encoder would likely improve trait prediction, but it would also require more careful controls to avoid leaking label information into transfer and discovery analyses. For that reason, the current split between an unsupervised shared encoder and supervised downstream probes is a reasonable design.

[normal] It is important to note that the key novelty of this research is neither in the finding of the best-performing predictor nor in any other particular result; rather, it is in providing a well-rounded workflow that allows us to integrate diverse leaf spectrometry data into one framework. The findings show that raw spectra perform best in regression tasks of importance and embedding – in alignment and exploration. That division of labor is scientifically useful: it allows the pipeline to support both prediction and hypothesis generation without conflating the two. The next step is likely not simply adding more datasets but introducing a representation objective that is more trait-aware while still preserving the cross-dataset structure that makes discovery possible.

[normal] CONCLUSION

[normal] This study demonstrates that hyperspectral leaf reflectance can be organized into a shared latent space that supports both trait prediction and candidate discovery across heterogeneous EcoSIS datasets. Raw spectra were found to be the best choice in most regression tasks, but the learned latent representation proved to be a good alternative in case of certain pigment- and chemistry-related traits and was helpful in cross-dataset analysis and novelty ranking. In addition, it was shown how the model’s performance depends on dataset size, trait structure and measurement regime; larger datasets yielded the clearest trait signals, whereas smaller field datasets served as robustness stress-tests. The cross-domain experiments showed that dataset identity remains strongly encoded in both raw spectra and embeddings; the embedding therefore provides a shared coordinate system for comparing heterogeneous datasets rather than a domain-invariant representation, and its behavior on datasets unseen during encoder training remains to be established.
